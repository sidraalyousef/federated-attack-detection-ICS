## Part 1 recap — Testbed/Dataset papers (already covered)

**[1] Mathur & Tippenhauer — "SWaT: A Water Treatment Testbed for Research and Training on ICS Security"**
Describes the SWaT testbed built at SUTD: a scaled-down (5 US gallons/hr) but fully operational 6-stage water treatment plant, each stage run by a redundant pair of PLCs over an EtherNet/IP ring network, with a central SCADA/historian. It is the de facto benchmark used by nearly every other paper in your pile — contribution is the testbed + dataset itself (7 days normal, 4 days with 36 attacks), not a detection method.

**[3] Shin, Lee, Yun & Kim — "HAI 1.0: HIL-based Augmented ICS Security Dataset"**
Introduces the HAI testbed/dataset, which couples a GE turbine, Emerson boiler and a FESTO water-treatment process through a dSPACE hardware-in-the-loop simulator to emulate an interconnected power-generation + water system. Contribution is a richer, more realistic multi-process dataset (10 days normal / 5.5 days with 38 attacks) with "stealth" attacks that conceal their effect on sensor readings and more reliable automated labeling than hand-labeled sets like SWaT.

## Part 2 — Deep centralized anomaly detection

**[6] Inoue, Yamagata, Chen, Poskitt & Sun — "Anomaly Detection for a Water Treatment System Using Unsupervised Machine Learning Methods" (LSTM/DNN vs. one-class SVM on SWaT)**
Compares two unsupervised detectors on SWaT: (1) a DNN trained as a *probabilistic outlier detector* — an LSTM reads the log history and predicts the probability distribution of the next actuator/sensor reading; low-probability observations are flagged via a summed "outlier factor" built from cross-entropy; (2) a one-class SVM with an RBF kernel run over sliding windows of raw sensor/actuator values. Contribution: an early, carefully-controlled head-to-head of a deep sequence model against a classical shallow baseline on the same real ICS dataset. Findings: DNN gets higher precision (0.98) and SVM higher recall (0.70 vs 0.68), giving the DNN a slightly better F-measure (0.80 vs 0.80), but the DNN costs far more to train (~2 weeks vs. 30 min) and to run (8h vs 10min on the test set). Both models struggle with gradual sensor drifts and attacks that only manipulate actuator *behavior* rather than raw values — a limitation that motivates the graph- and GAN-based models below.

**[10] Li, Chen, Jiang, Liu & Buja — "MAD-GAN: Multivariate Anomaly Detection for Time Series Data with Generative Adversarial Networks"**
Proposes MAD-GAN: an LSTM-RNN generator and an LSTM-RNN discriminator are trained adversarially (GAN) purely on normal multivariate time-series windows, so the generator learns the full spatio-temporal correlation structure of the sensors/actuators as a whole rather than channel-by-channel. At test time it introduces **DR-score**, an anomaly score that combines (a) a *discrimination* loss from the discriminator's real/fake probability and (b) a *reconstruction* loss from mapping a test window back into the generator's latent space (via gradient-descent-based inversion) and comparing the generated output to the real window. Contribution: one of the first GAN-based detectors that models all sensors/actuators jointly (capturing inter-sensor correlations other methods miss) and validated on SWaT and WADI, reporting improved precision/recall over earlier LSTM/SVM/PCA/KNN baselines, especially for attacks that only become visible as a cross-sensor inconsistency.

**[11] Audibert, Michiardi, Guyard, Marti & Zuluaga — "USAD: UnSupervised Anomaly Detection on Multivariate Time Series"**
Proposes USAD, an autoencoder architecture with **one shared encoder and two decoders**. Training has two phases: first both autoencoders are trained normally to reconstruct normal data; then they are trained adversarially — one decoder tries to fool the other into mis-classifying its reconstruction as original input, while the other tries to correctly tell real from reconstructed. This adversarial training (inspired by GANs but without a true GAN's instability) amplifies the reconstruction error specifically on anomalous inputs at inference time, and a tunable parameter lets you trade off false positives vs. detection speed/sensitivity. Contribution: fast, stable training (no separate discriminator network, no mode-collapse issues typical of full GANs) while still getting GAN-like sensitivity to anomalies, and strong empirical results across five public datasets including SWaT. USAD later becomes a very common federated/centralized baseline in the FedAnomaly, GDN, and survey papers below.

**[12] Deng & Hooi — "Graph Neural Network-Based Anomaly Detection in Multivariate Time Series" (GDN, evaluated on SWaT)**
Proposes GDN (Graph Deviation Network): rather than treating sensors independently, GDN first **learns a sensor-relationship graph** (via embedding similarity, not given a priori) that captures which sensors/actuators are structurally related; a graph-attention layer then predicts each sensor's expected value from its learned neighbors; large deviations between predicted and observed values (weighted/normalized per-sensor) flag anomalies, and attention weights over the learned graph give a degree of **explainability** (which related sensors drove the alert). Contribution: explicit, learned structural/relational modeling (vs. implicit correlation-learning in MAD-GAN/USAD) plus interpretability, and strong precision/recall/F1 on SWaT and WADI versus PCA, KNN, AE, LSTM-VAE, DAGMM and MAD-GAN baselines — it is repeatedly used as the strongest non-federated baseline in later papers (e.g., it tops Table 2 of the "Evaluation" paper below, and is a benchmark in FedAnomaly).

**[14] Ayas, Ayas, Cavdar & Sahin — "Detecting Cyberattacks Based on Deep Neural Network Approaches in Industrial Control Systems"**
Systematically compares four *simple* deep architectures — CNN, RNN, LSTM and GRU — all used as **forecasters** (predict next sensor/actuator values from a 30-step window) rather than as reconstructors, on both SWaT and WADI. Anomalies are then flagged with a lightweight statistical layer on top: per-feature z-scores of the prediction error, combined with a 10-step sliding window so an alert fires only when the z-score stays elevated, avoiding single-timestep false alarms. Contribution: shows that GRU gives the lowest prediction error of the four architectures, while plain RNN achieves the best detection F1 (0.9848 on SWaT, 0.7651 on WADI) — i.e., simple, computationally cheap DNNs combined with careful statistical thresholding can match or beat many of the more complex GAN/GNN methods surveyed in their related-work table (which itself is a useful one-stop summary of ~30 centralized SWaT/WADI detectors, e.g., MAD-GAN, GDN, DIF, LW-LSTM-VAE, PGRGAT, EGNN). Good citation if you want one table summarizing "who has used what model on SWaT/WADI."

**[16] Kim, Choi, Choi, Lee & Yoon — "Towards a Rigorous Evaluation of Time-Series Anomaly Detection" (AAAI-22)**
Not a new detector — a methodological critique of how papers like the ones above are evaluated. It shows mathematically and empirically that the widely-used **point-adjustment (PA)** protocol (if any point in a true anomaly segment is detected, the whole segment counts as detected) can make even a *random* anomaly score look like a state-of-the-art detector, because PA inflates recall while barely touching precision. It also shows an *untrained*, randomly-initialized reconstruction model gets suspiciously competitive F1 scores on several benchmarks (including SWaT/WADI), and proposes two fixes: a **random-score / untrained-model baseline** that any real method must clearly beat, and a new protocol, **PA%K**, which only grants the PA credit to a segment if at least K% of it was actually detected. Contribution: directly relevant as a critical-appraisal section for your lit review — it re-ranks USAD, DAGMM, LSTM-VAE, OmniAnomaly, MSCRED, THOC and **GDN** on SWaT/WADI without PA, and finds GDN is the only method that consistently beats the untrained baseline across datasets, while several headline "SOTA" F1 scores reported elsewhere essentially evaporate once PA is removed. Worth flagging if any of your chosen centralized/federated papers report only F1-with-PA.

## Part 3 — Federated attack/anomaly detection

**[19] Li, Wu, Nagarajan, Lin, Tahir & Bartlett (NEC/UConn group) — "DeepFed: Federated Deep Learning for Intrusion Detection in Industrial Cyber-Physical Systems"**
Proposes DeepFed, generally credited as one of the first federated IDS designed specifically for ICS. Each client trains a hybrid **CNN + Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)** model locally (CNN extracts spatial/feature correlations, GRU captures temporal dependencies) and only model gradients/weights are shared; aggregation uses a FedAvg-style scheme, and the paper adds a secure, **Paillier-homomorphic-encryption-based aggregation protocol** so the server never sees plaintext gradients, defending against honest-but-curious servers/clients. Contribution: establishes the standard "CNN+GRU local model + secure FedAvg aggregation" template for federated ICS intrusion detection that several later papers (Fed-ExDNN, FED-SCADA) position themselves against, and demonstrates it is both accurate and more privacy-preserving than centralized training on industrial CPS traffic.

**[20] Zhang, Jiang, Severesky, Xu, Liu & Song — "Federated Variational Learning for Anomaly Detection in Multivariate Time Series" (FedAnomaly)**
Proposes **FedAnomaly**, which combines a Variational Autoencoder with a Convolutional Gated Recurrent Unit (**ConvGRU-VAE**) to jointly capture feature-level and temporal dependencies in multivariate time series, trained only on normal data at the edge, then aggregated via federated learning (standard FedAvg-style rounds) so no raw data leaves each client/entity. Contribution: (1) the first federated *deep generative* model for time-series anomaly detection (prior federated anomaly work was mostly predictive/discriminative); (2) ConvGRU is a novel way to fold convolution into GRU gates so the encoder captures cross-feature correlations, not just temporal order; (3) extensive experiments (non-federated vs. federated, varying window length, hidden size, number of clients, local epochs) on SMAP/MSL/SWaT showing their non-federated ConvGRU-VAE beats USAD, GDN, OmniAnomaly, DAGMM, MAD-GAN, LSTM-VAE and AE outright on F1, while the federated version (FedAnomaly) trades some accuracy for privacy but still clears 0.8+ F1 on SWaT and is competitive with (sometimes better than) non-federated baselines. Useful source for how to actually run and report a federated-vs-centralized ablation (client count, local-epoch count, latency, communication rounds) — directly applicable to your own SWaT/WADI federated experiments.

**[21] Zhu, Song, Chen, Cheng, Zong, Mizoguchi, Lumezanu, Chen & Luo — "Fed-ExDNN: Deep Federated Anomaly Detection for Multivariate Time Series Data"**
Identifies a specific failure mode of naively combining FedAvg with unsupervised anomaly detection: each edge device's normal data may only cover *some* of the normal "modes" (e.g., one elderly user's device only sees "walking/sitting/standing", never "running/cycling"), so federated averaging either collapses to a narrow normal-space or mis-labels unseen-but-normal behavior as anomalous. They propose **Fed-ExDNN**: an Exemplar-based Deep Neural Network (ExDNN) where each client jointly learns an LSTM feature encoder *and* a small set of learnable "exemplars" (cluster-like prototypes, trained via a Deep-Embedding-Clustering-style KL loss plus a "Deep Relative Preserving" term) representing the normal patterns it has seen; on the server, a **Federated Constrained Clustering (FedCC)** step aligns and merges the different clients' exemplars (instead of naively averaging weights) into a single global exemplar set that covers the union of normal modes, which is then redistributed to all edges. Contribution: formalizes "Federated Unsupervised Anomaly Detection" (FedUAD) as a distinct problem from supervised federated learning and shows ExDNN/Fed-ExDNN outperform both standard unsupervised detectors and standard federated-learning aggregation (FedAvg/FedProx/etc.) on six public multivariate time-series datasets, specifically because it handles **heterogeneous (non-IID) normal-pattern distribution** across clients — a problem your own SWaT/WADI split across multiple simulated clients will also face.

**[23] Nassar & Al-Mashagba — "FED-SCADA: A Trustworthy and Energy-efficient Federated IDS for Smart Grid Edge Gateways Using SNNs and Differential Evolution"**
Proposes FED-SCADA for resource-constrained SCADA edge gateways in smart grids: each client trains a lightweight **Spiking Neural Network (SNN)** locally (event-driven, leaky-integrate-and-fire neurons, much lower energy per inference than CNN/LSTM), and instead of FedAvg, the server aggregates client models using **Differential Evolution (DE)** — treating each client's weights as one member of a DE population and using mutation/crossover/selection to produce a new global model, which the paper argues converges better than FedAvg under non-IID client data and needs no gradient assumptions. Evaluated on TON_IoT, Edge-IIoTset and **SWaT**, reporting 96.4% accuracy, 28 ms inference latency and 1.1 mJ/sample energy use, beating FedAvg-CNN and FedSVM baselines on accuracy, latency, energy and a defined "privacy leakage" metric. Contribution: targets the energy/latency axis that most federated-ICS papers ignore, and is the only paper in your set combining neuromorphic computing (SNNs) with evolutionary (non-gradient) federated aggregation — worth noting, though flag for your methodology section that the paper's Table-1 baselines/metrics are explicitly stated to be synthesized/reproduced from cited prior studies rather than all independently re-run from scratch, so treat its absolute numbers a bit more cautiously than the peer-reviewed venues above.

**[27] Makris, Karampasi, Radoglou-Grammatikis, Episkopos, Iturbe, Rios, Piperigkos, Lalos, Xenakis, Lagkas, Argyriou & Sarigiannidis — "A Comprehensive Survey of Federated Intrusion Detection Systems: Techniques, Challenges and Solutions"**
A 2025 survey of the federated-IDS literature as a whole (not SWaT/WADI-specific). It (1) reviews FL fundamentals relevant to IDS — model- vs. data-centric FL, cross-device vs. cross-silo, horizontal vs. vertical FL, and the main aggregation strategies (FedAvg/FedSGD, FedProx, FedDANE, FedOpt); (2) systematically tabulates ~60+ federated IDS papers across IT networks, cyber-physical systems/IoT, Industrial IoT, smart grids, Internet of Vehicles and Internet of Medical Things, recording each one's target system, fusion/aggregation technique, protocols, attack types, datasets and reported accuracy; (3) distills cross-cutting challenges (data privacy vs. model robustness trade-offs, non-IID heterogeneity, communication overhead) and future directions. Contribution to your review: the single best "state of the field" citation to frame *why* federated attack detection matters and *where the open problems are* (exactly the heterogeneity/non-IID problem that Fed-ExDNN and FedAnomaly also wrestle with), and its Table 1 is a ready-made cross-reference if you want to argue your SWaT/WADI infrastructure-attack-detection project sits in an under-covered niche relative to the IoT/smart-grid-heavy literature it catalogs.
